# Perspective Correction

Keeping verticals straight so buildings don't look like they're falling over.

## Why verticals converge

- Tilting the camera up (to fit a tall building) or down (to include a floor) tilts the sensor plane off the facade. Parallel lines then converge — **keystoning**.
- The fix is always the same principle: **keep the sensor plane parallel to the facade** and shoot level.

## In-camera fixes (best — do it here)

- Shoot **level**. Electronic/bubble level front-to-back and side-to-side.
- Keep the **sensor parallel to the facade** — square-on for one-point.
- Need more height? **Step back and shoot wider**, then crop up — instead of tilting.
- Use a **taller tripod** or raise the camera to re-centre the subject without tilt.
- **Tilt-shift lens with rise** — the pro solution: raise the lens's image circle to include the top of a building while the camera stays dead level. No convergence, no post stretch.

## Post fixes

- **Lightroom Transform** — Auto, Guided (draw 2–4 reference lines on verticals/horizontals), or the Vertical/Level sliders. Guided is the workhorse for architecture.
- **Photoshop** — Adaptive Wide Angle (fix lens bow + verticals), Perspective Crop tool, or free-transform on a converted layer.
- Correct verticals first, then fine-tune with manual sliders.

## Trade-off: shoot wider

- Correction **stretches and crops** the frame — pixels near edges get pushed out and the top corners get cut.
- So **shoot wider than the final** and leave headroom/side room. Budget ~10–20% margin for the correction.

## When NOT to fully correct

- Very **tall towers** shot from close: fully-corrected verticals can look unnaturally stretched/wide at the top. A touch of residual keystone reads more natural.
- Deliberate up-angle hero shots — correcting kills the drama. Judgement call.
- Always level the **horizon** regardless.
