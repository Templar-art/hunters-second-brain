# Focus Stacking

Blend multiple frames focused at different distances into one image sharp front-to-back.

## When you need it
- Tight interiors where near foreground (bench, table edge) AND a distant window/wall must both be sharp — even f/11 won't hold the depth.
- Foreground-to-window compositions with strong near objects.
- Detail / product-style shots (fixtures, joinery) at close range where DOF is razor thin.
- Avoiding diffraction: stopping to f/22 softens the whole frame on full frame. Stack instead of stopping down past ~f/11–f/16.

## How to shoot
- Tripod, locked composition. No movement.
- Manual focus. Manual exposure.
- Shoot a series stepping focus from nearest point to farthest, with generous overlap in the sharp zones.
- Vary FOCUS ONLY — keep aperture, shutter, ISO, WB fixed across the set.
- Use the lens's sharp midrange (~f/5.6–f/8) per frame, not f/22. More frames at f/8 beats one soft f/22 grab.
- 3–6 frames covers most interiors; more for macro detail.

## Blending
- Photoshop: load files as stack (Scripts > Load Files into Stack), auto-align layers, then Edit > Auto-Blend Layers > "Stack Images". Flatten, clean edges.
- Helicon Focus / Zerene for tricky micro-detail — better halo control.

## Pitfalls
- Moving subjects (curtains, plants, people) leave ghosts — reshoot or mask.
- Focus breathing changes framing slightly as focus shifts; auto-align handles small shifts, leave crop margin.
- Halos/artifacts at high-contrast edges — retouch by hand or feather-mask the good frame in.

## With brackets
- Stacking + [[exposure-blending]]: either shoot each focus step as a full bracket then stack per-exposure, or blend HDR first then stack the blended results. Fewer total frames if the window is the only bright zone — bracket just the frames that see it.
