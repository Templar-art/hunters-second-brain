The approach

Let people see it,
not hear it.

Every idea here is built so the viewer finds something out by watching: a pick made with the logos hidden, a count, a guess you can get wrong, a hat that gets worn for real. You're the person reacting, deciding and calling it, not reading lines.

Logos hidden where it mattersIn the comparison videos nobody knows which hat is yours until the reveal. That's what makes the result believable.
Results go out as filmedIf a pick, a count or a guess doesn't go our way, it still posts. You sign off on that before we lock anything.
Nobody gets linesParticipants get neutral questions only. Your opening lines are starting points; say them your way or not at all.
Small tests called smallOne person, one day, informal. Every hat is set the way its wearer likes it, on camera.

01

Red or Blue?

Pillar

Side by Side Comparison

The question

With the logos hidden, which hat would you wear? And does seeing the side change the answer the front gave?

How it works

A blind pick with a turn, across two posts.

  • Both hats get identical grey tape over the front panel, plus a red or a blue dot.
  • The viewer and the wearer pick from the front first. Then the locked side and back views land, and the wearer is asked again.
  • Part 1 resolves the wearer's turn and ends on the viewers' vote. Part 2 takes the tape off.
First two seconds

A stacked 9:16 split, front-on: the same face twice, in two caps with matching grey tape over the fronts, a red dot on one tape and a blue dot on the other. On screen: “Red or blue?” No voice.

Opening line

None from Shak. The first words heard are the producer's prompt to the wearer, off camera: “Which one would you actually wear out?”

Payoff

Part 1: whether the side changed the wearer's pick. Part 2: which one was LRGR, and how Shak takes a pick that may not be his. If the wearer and the vote both land on the other hat, that's the post.

Why people watch

You pick before the turn and before the brand. The logos are hidden, so the brand doesn't steer the pick, though anyone who already knows the A-frame's shape may still recognise it. It tests the proposal's central idea (“Show the side, not the front”) instead of asserting it, and it might not hold. Part 2 is also where Shak responds to someone whose preference may differ from his.

Difficulty

Medium.

02

Ours Or Not?

Pillar

Side by Side Comparison

The question

Could the person behind this hat pick it out without looking? And what is he actually feeling for?

How it works

A feel box, with the viewer one step ahead of Shak.

  • The cardboard box has its back cut away towards the camera. Shak's hands come in through two holes on the far side.
  • A hat slides in. The viewer sees it, and an on-screen tag gives them the answer before Shak calls “ours” or “not ours”.
  • A coin toss decides each round and hats can repeat, so he can't count his way to the answer.
  • Six rounds that get harder on a fixed schedule, scored against luck. Six from six by guessing is 1 in 64. Five or better is about 1 in 9.
First two seconds

Front-on: a plain box on a table, its open back facing the lens. Two hands push in through holes on the far side as a cap slides in from the left. A small tag above it reads “LRGR” or “NOT”.

Opening line

“Mix 'em up. I've just got to say if it's one of ours.”

Payoff

The score against chance, and the one detail his hands relied on, found blind rather than claimed. A miss gets shown.

Why people watch

You know the answer while he doesn't, so every hesitation and wrong call lands. The founder can fail on his own channel, and the fixed schedule means the last rounds are the hardest whether he's winning or not. What he feels for is something you can check by hand in a shop.

Difficulty

Low.

The One I Knocked Back

Pillar

Side by Side Comparison (with a Built For Us decision behind it)

The question

Can you even see the thing he was fussy about? And would a buyer have cared?

How it works

A participant spots the difference, then gives an honest answer.

  • Two near-identical hats, with no markings.
  • A participant tries to find what changed, and the viewer looks along with them.
  • Shak shows the difference, with the evidence.
  • Then he asks “Would you have noticed?” and has to respond to whatever comes back.
First two seconds

Close on the participant's hands turning both hats over on a plain surface, with no tape and no markings. Off screen, Shak: “One of these I sent back.”

Opening line

(Shak, off screen) “One of these I sent back.”

Payoff

The difference, proof that it happened, and the clash or agreement between his standard and a buyer's answer. A flat “no” stays in, and his response to it is the ending.

Why people watch

You look for it alongside the participant, then watch a founder defend being fussy to someone who might not care.

Difficulty

Low, if the sample exists.

04

The Tug Count

Pillar

The Hat Test

The question

How often do you reach up to fix your cap without noticing? The research describes people who keep pulling their caps down. This counts it for one person instead of claiming it.

How it works

A running tally where the viewer knows which hat is which, and the wearer and the official counter don't.

  • Four 15-minute blocks of something the wearer genuinely does, filmed on one locked wide in the order A-B-B-A, so neither hat always gets the later slot. A coin toss decides which hat is A.
  • Both hats stay taped, tagged “1” and “2”.
  • The LRGR helper clicks live, for the sound and the picture. The helper knows which hat is which, so the live count isn't the official one.
  • Afterwards, someone who has never seen LRGR hats, this brief or the hypothesis recounts from the footage. That's the official number.
  • The viewer sees the hats labelled from the first block. There's no brand reveal.
First two seconds

Captured live in block 1 on a second phone locked over the clicker's shoulder: the tally counter sharp in the foreground, the wearer soft behind. A hand goes up to the hat and the thumb clicks, loud in the mix. If block 1 doesn't give a clean moment, open on the locked wide with the click audible and a counter overlay. No re-enacted tugs.

Opening line

(Shak to the wearer, at the start of block 1) “Every time you touch it, that's a click.”

Payoff

Four raw numbers against the margin set in advance, whichever way they fall. A difference inside the margin is a draw. On screen: “One person, one hour, informal.”

Why people watch

It's a scoreboard you can count along with. You know which hat is which, and the wearer and the person who did the official count don't. It also puts the wearer's Red or Blue? pick next to what their hands actually did.

Difficulty

Medium.

05

In The Bag

Pillar

The Hat Test

The question

Can I take my cap off, shove it in my bag and get the same hat back out? (Creative hypothesis: caps spend a lot of their life off the head.)

How it works

A before-and-after through one real bag, compared with an overlay wipe.

  • Two new hats (LRGR and a second copy of the comparison cap) are shot on a gridded board from fixed positions.
  • The bag's owner packs both their normal way. Halfway through, they swap the two hats' places.
  • At the end, the hats come out onto the same marks, and the edit wipes the after frame over the before.
  • Ten minutes later, there's one more frame, with the hats untouched.
First two seconds

Overhead: two caps on a gridded cutting mat beside the owner's open bag. The owner's hand picks one up and packs it into their own bag the way they normally do.

Opening line

(to the bag's owner) “Pack them how you normally would.”

Payoff

A dent, a crease or nothing, and whether it comes back. LRGR may come out worse, and that stays in.

Why people watch

The wipe answers the question in a second. It uses the owner's real bag and their real way of packing, not a lab stunt.

Difficulty

Low.

06

What Goes First?

Pillar

The Hat Test

The question

Which part of a cap gives out first when you actually wear it? And does the person behind it know?

How it works

A bet in plain view, with an untouched control hat.

  • Two hats from one confirmed batch. One is sealed in a box with Shak's signature across the tape. The other is worn for 30 logged wears.
  • Shak and the wearer each write down what they think goes first, and roughly when, and read it to camera. The cards go on screen, then into an envelope taped to the box. The envelope locks the predictions in. It doesn't hide them, so the viewer can take a side.
  • Checkpoints at wears 10 and 20.
  • At wear 30, the box is cut open, the twins go side by side under the Ep. 0 setups, and the envelope is opened.
First two seconds

A two-shot: Shak and the wearer on either side of a taped, signed box, each holding a card up to the camera. On screen: “WEAR 0 / 30”.

Opening line

“That's mine. No changing it at thirty.”

Payoff

Ep. 0's payoff is the bet itself, out in the open. At wear 30: a worn hat next to its untouched twin, and whose call was right. No visible change is also a result.

Why people watch

Two calls you can see and side with, and a counter that moves. The finale compares against a hat you watched get sealed, not a memory of day one.

Difficulty

Low on the day (about 45 minutes).

The Drawer Hat

Pillar

Streetwear Built For Us

The question
How it works

A specific call before the evidence, then one fix.

  • The owner puts on a hat they stopped wearing. Shak looks it over and writes one specific guess on a card before they say a word.
  • The card flips, and the owner gives the real reason.
  • Shak gets one fix without swapping the hat: the strap, how it's seated, the angle, or the brim (with permission).
  • The owner decides where it goes: back on their head or back in the drawer.
First two seconds

Mid-shot: the owner sits in their old cap, worn the way they used to wear it. Beside them, Shak writes on a card angled away from them. No LRGR hat on the table.

Opening line

“Don't tell me why yet.”

Payoff

Whether Shak called it, and the owner's decision. “Keep your hat, wear it like this” is a valid ending, and maybe the most believable one the brand can post.

Why people watch

You guess along with him, the owner's reason might be yours, and he can be wrong in front of them. It's the slate's long conversation, where the talk is the point.

Difficulty

Low per episode: a table, two chairs, the main camera plus a locked phone on the wide, two lavs, and the Red or Blue? profile set left standing for the before-and-after.

Shak's Bet

Pillar

Streetwear Built For Us

The question

Does the person behind this hat know who it's not for? And what does he say when someone wants something different?

How it works

A hidden bet, then a live disagreement.

  • Three people, each in their own everyday cap, chat with Shak one at a time.
  • They step out. Shak puts a coin under one of three upturned cups at their seats, for the person he reckons won't want the LRGR hat, and gives his reason to camera in one sentence.
  • They come back. All three try the hat, set it their way and give a verdict. The cup is lifted only after the last verdict.
  • Shak asks whoever's least keen “If we did one for you, what's different?”, then has to say whether he'd do it.
First two seconds

Three people sit down in a row, each in a different cap of their own, each behind an upturned cup. Nobody has spoken yet.

Opening line

“One of you won't want this. I've already picked who.”

Payoff

Hit or miss, the reasons people actually gave, and a founder answering someone who wants something else. It ends on the limit or the decision, never on a pitch.

Why people watch

A founder bets against his own product and might be wrong, then has to deal with someone who doesn't want what he sells.

Difficulty

Low–medium.

09

Will It Do Up?

Pillar

Streetwear Built For Us

The question

Will it even do up on my head? And does head size tell you where you'll actually set the strap? (The fit discussions behind the research come from threads about large heads. That's a creative lead, not audience data.)

How it works

The tape's call against the person's own choice.

  • The ladder: the hat's inside circumference is measured at every notch on camera, with the method shown, and the figures are stacked into a ladder.
  • The call: each person's head is measured, and the ladder calls the notch closest to that measurement. The call goes on screen for the viewer. The person doesn't hear it.
  • The result: the person sets the strap how they like it. A call counts only if it's the exact notch they choose.
  • Score: “The tape called [x] of [n].”
  • Last notch: whoever's nearest the end of the range tries the last notch.
First two seconds

Extreme close-up on the back of a head: two thumbs pressing a snapback's last peg towards its hole. On screen: “Last notch.” Cut before it closes. If nobody is near the end of the range: the ladder building one notch at a time, the tape laid round the inside of the sweatband and each figure stacking on screen.

Opening line

“Don't put it on yet. Tape first.”

Payoff

“The tape called [x] of [n]”, the range in centimetres, and what the score showed on these heads, whichever way it went. The closing line is written in the edit from what was filmed. The end card lists each person's rounded head size, their notch and their own words, under “Rough guide only. Same size doesn't mean same shape.”

Why people watch

You guess along with the tape, you get a real number for a practical question, and there's a moment of suspense at the last notch.

Difficulty

Medium with four to six people.

Mark It Up

Pillar

Streetwear Built For Us

The question

Would the founder change anything about his own hat? And would someone who's worn it pick the same things?

How it works

Three calls each, then a second opinion.

  • Shak pulls a hat from a stock box without looking.
  • He gets three strips of tape in total, in any mix: orange for “I'd change this”, green for “not touching it”. He chooses where they go, so he has to decide what matters. One sentence per strip.
  • Then the wearer marks up the LRGR hat they wore today, with the tape removed, under the same three-strip limit and out of his sight.
  • Both hats go side by side.
First two seconds

Macro: the first strip of tape going onto a hat on a turntable, Shak's thumb pressing it down. No timer, no voice.

Opening line

“Three strips. Orange I'd change, green I'm not touching.”

Payoff

Two marked-up hats in one frame, three strips each, showing where the founder and a wearer agree and where they don't. How many of his three are orange is the honest verdict.

Why people watch

A founder marking his own product as flawed is uncommon, three strips force him to choose, the opinion reads in a single frame, and the second opinion gives the video a turn.

Difficulty

Low.

Can't Unsee It

Pillar

Streetwear Built For Us

The question

What's the thing on a cap that the person behind a hat brand can't stop seeing?

How it works

A ten-second spot-it loop that only the viewer plays.

  • One held frame of a cap with the logo covered, and two silent seconds to look.
  • Then Shak's thumb lands on the detail, and he says why it bugs him.
  • LRGR's own hats are in the pool, covered like the rest. If an A-frame has one of his pet hates, that episode runs like any other.
First two seconds

A locked close-up of a cap on a plain surface, its logo under plain tape. No hands, no voice. On screen: “Spot it?”

Opening line

None set. After the pause he names the detail his own way.

Payoff

The reveal and his reason. You go and check your own cap.

Why people watch

A tiny game with the answer inside five seconds, built to loop. It leaves you with something to look for next time you're buying.

Difficulty

Low.

Shape First

Pillar

Streetwear Built For Us (drop and range film)

The question

What's the new one?

How it works

A backlit row of identical hat silhouettes on stands. The shape is the constant, and the hero.

  • A front light clicks on one hat at a time, in release order.
  • The last silhouette stays dark a beat longer. That's the new one.
  • Each drop adds a stand, so the film becomes a running record of the range.
  • The hats are on stands, not heads, so the film makes no fit claim.
First two seconds

A white backlit wall, a row of black hat silhouettes on stands, and silence. A switch clicks, and the first hat lights up in colour.

Opening line

None at the open; the switch clicks carry it. Shak's only line comes at the end, in his own words: which one's new, plus his real reason for the colour, if he has one.

Payoff

The new colour, revealed in line with everything before it, plus one honest line.

Why people watch

The pattern sets up the wait for the last reveal, and at 8–15 seconds it's made to replay. Instead of a stock trailer, it leads with the shape the public product descriptions emphasise (the deep crown and curved brim) and keeps it constant, so colour is the only thing that changes.

Difficulty

Medium.

Shoot plan

Red or Blue?

The idea

Someone who has never seen an LRGR hat picks between two caps with every logo taped over. They pick twice and get the same question both times. The first pick is front on, in a mirror. The second comes after they see locked side and back frames of both hats, stacked on a laptop screen. Part 1 shows both picks and asks the viewer the same question as a vote. Part 2 recaps the picks and the vote, then films Shak hearing the picks for the first time, peeling the tape himself and responding. It also discloses the comparison cap and how it was bought.

The video tests the proposal's line, "Show the side, not the front," on one head instead of stating it. If seeing the side changes nothing, that is the result, and we publish it.

Viewer takeaway - Part 1: whether seeing the side and back changed one person's pick, and which hat the viewer would pick. - Part 2: which hat was LRGR, whether the wearer and the viewers agreed, and how the founder takes an answer that may not go his way.

Opening

Part 1 - Shot: a stacked 9:16 split made from the locked 0° frames of each hat's first pass. - The same face in the same position in both halves, eyes on the target beside the lens. The taped patches and coloured dots are clearly visible. - The red-dot hat is always in the top half and the blue-dot hat in the bottom, in every split in both parts. - A thin neutral divider separates the halves. Each half has a small red or blue chip in its top corner, so the halves can still be told apart in the side and back frames, where the dots don't show. - Action: none. The frame is still for about 2s. - Dialogue: at about 1s, the producer's prompt from the mirror round, off camera: "Which one would you wear? Why?" These are the exact words on the prompt card, used in both rounds. - On screen: "Red or blue?" centred on the divider from frame one. Burned-in captions for the prompt.

Part 2 - Shot: close on the two taped hats on the stool. Shak's thumb and finger pinch the corner of the tape on one of them. Cut before the tape lifts and any logo shows. This is live footage from the start of the real peel, not a re-enactment. - Dialogue: room audio only, as recorded. - On screen: "Red or blue? Part 2".

Shak's role
  • During the picks:
  • He stays out of the room and out of earshot, and doesn't see the laptop.
  • Nobody tells him the picks.
  • He doesn't see the comparison cap before the shoot.
  • At The Tug Count briefing, he doesn't handle or name either hat.
  • At the tape-off:
  • He talks to the wearer, not the lens.
  • He hears the picks with the tape still on and can ask why.
  • He peels the tape himself, then responds.
  • After that, he looks at the side split on the laptop. It's his first look at the frames.
  • Tone: no performance. He talks to the wearer the way he would to anyone trying on his hats.
  • If the pick isn't his hat, he says what he actually thinks. He can agree, disagree, ask more or concede.
  • No recovery pitch, and he doesn't explain the result with product features the video can't show.
  • He talks about how each hat sat on this person, as filmed. Nothing about the other brand beyond that.
  • Recognising his own hat: he may recognise it under the tape. If he does, saying so on camera is fine.
  • Starting points. He says these his own way, or not at all:
  • "Which one'd you go for?"
  • "What was it about that one?"
  • "What'd you see from the side?"
  • Off-camera questions from the producer, after the peel:
  • "What do you make of that?"
  • "Did you know which one was yours before the tape came off?"
  • (At the laptop) "What do you notice about how each one sits on them?"
  • "Would you have picked the same, on their head?"
  • "Seeing that, is there anything you'd want to change on yours?"

The Tug Count

The idea

One wearer spends an hour doing something they actually do, split into four 15-minute blocks. They wear the two taped caps from Red or Blue? in A-B-B-A order, and a coin toss on camera decides which cap is A. Each time a hand moves or re-seats the cap, the helper clicks a tally counter. The viewer is told which cap is LRGR from the start. The wearer isn't told, and the official counter doesn't know either: they recount later from muted footage with no idea which cap is which.

The edit plays like a scoreboard. A strip across the top fills one block at a time. A half-time score appears after block 2. The second half runs the same caps the other way round, so the viewer gets a provisional answer in the middle and then sees whether it holds. The video ends on full-time totals, judged against a draw margin Shak wrote and signed before anyone was counted.

Why someone stops: a thumb clicking a counter as a hand goes up to a cap is a habit most cap wearers recognise straight away. The question (which cap gets fewer touches?) is set up in about 10 seconds and doesn't resolve until the last card.

Takeaway: how often this one person moved or re-seated each of these two caps over two 15-minute blocks each of their usual [activity]. That sits next to the wearer's own guess and the cap they picked in Red or Blue?. The research context mentions people who keep pulling their caps down. This video counts that once, for one person, labelled informal. It is not a verdict on comfort, fit or quality, and on its own it says nothing about crown depth.

  • On green strips, say why you'd leave it or what you decided, not what the part does. Leave out materials, how it's made, and anything about it being "better", unless it's confirmed.
  • If he moves a strip because he's changed his mind, that stays in. What we won't do is redo a strip for a better shot.
  • Only mention a change he's making if it's real. No dates, no hints at a future product.
  • He won't see the wearer's hat until the cover lifts, and once it lifts nobody touches a strip. He shouldn't tell the wearer what he marked. At the tape-off and over lunch, he shouldn't talk them through what he likes or would change about the hat.
  • Suggested line, a starting point only: "Three strips. Orange, I'd change it. Green, I'd leave it." He says it his way, or the legend carries it.
  • Off-camera questions (from the producer):
  • "What's the first thing you look at?"
  • If he stalls, ask whichever question he hasn't covered yet, so the prompts don't steer the mix: "What wouldn't you touch? Why?" or "What would you change if you could? Why?"
  • For each strip, only if he hasn't said: "Why that one?"
  • At the reveal: "What did they mark that you didn't? Agree or not?"
  • If he agrees with one of their orange strips: "Would you change it, or leave it?"
  • If he says he's changing something: "Is that decided, or a maybe?" A "maybe" stays a "maybe".
Payoff and ending

The payoff is the cover lifting: two hats side by side, three strips each, showing where the founder and someone who has worn the hat agree and where they don't. Because the viewer has already seen Shak's strips, the reveal belongs to the two people at the table. Their first look and Shak's response are the new information. The honest number is how many of Shak's three strips are orange, stated plainly in the tally.

The video ends on the top-down hold with the tally. The last sound is the tail of the last real line, or room tone. No end card, no product line, no question to camera. If someone will answer comments, the post caption can carry one plain question, for example "What would your three be on your own cap?" If Shak places zero orange strips, see J.

The Drawer Hat

The idea

Someone brings a cap they bought and then stopped wearing. With the camera already rolling, they take it out of their own bag and put it on the way they used to wear it. Before they say anything, Shak looks it over and writes one specific guess about why it ended up in the drawer. The owner gives their real reason first and then turns the card over. Shak gets one fix on their hat (the strap, how it's seated, the angle, or the brim with permission). He never swaps it for another hat. The owner then decides where it goes: back on their head or back in the bag.

Viewer takeaway: a reason a hat stops getting worn that the viewer may recognise, and an answer to whether it was the hat or the way it was worn. What's on show is Shak's eye, not his hat. LRGR appears only if the owner asks to try one. "Keep your hat, wear it like this" is a complete result.

Length: 75–90s. It's the slate's long conversation, and the reason and the flip both need room. It can run to about 100s if the owner asks to try an LRGR hat, because that stays in.

What Goes First?

The idea

Two LRGR hats come from one batch that LRGR confirms. The wearer picks them, and a coin decides which one gets worn. The other is sealed in a box with Shak's signature across the tape. That's the untouched control.

Before any wear, Shak and the wearer each write down which part they reckon goes first and roughly when. Each reads their card to camera. The cards go on screen and are then sealed in an envelope taped to the box. The predictions are public from Ep. 0, so the viewer can take a side. The envelope only stops anyone rewriting them.

The wearer logs 30 real wears and does checkpoints at 10 and 20 on a phone kit. At 30, the box is cut open and the worn hat goes next to its twin.

Takeaway: - Ep. 0: two specific calls on what wears first, one of them from the person behind the hat, and a set point when we find out. - Finale: what 30 logged wears did to one hat compared with its sealed twin, and whose call was right, even if neither was.

Opening
  • Shot: a locked vertical two-shot at chest height. Shak and the wearer stand shoulder to shoulder behind a table. The sealed box sits front and centre, with the signature across the tape facing the lens. Each holds an A5 prediction card at chest height, writing to camera. The cards have printed headers (GOES FIRST: / BY WEAR:) filled in with thick black marker in capitals, so they can be read on a phone.
  • Action: both cards are already up at frame one, and both people are still.
  • Suggested dialogue (Shak; a starting point he says his way, or not at all): “That's mine. No changing it at thirty.”
  • On-screen text: “WEAR 0 / 30” in the top third from frame one, in a counter style that every later episode reuses. No other text in the first two seconds, because the cards are the text.
  • Why it works before any explanation: two people, a sealed, signed box and two written calls you want to read. It reads as a bet before anyone says what the bet is.
